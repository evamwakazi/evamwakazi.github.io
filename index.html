How a Neural Network Learns: Six Building Blocks

A visual explainer built from my own neural network experiments. Six building blocks explain the whole machine: four of them — layers, neurons, weights, and activation functions — make a prediction possible; the last two — loss and optimization — are how the network actually learns from being wrong. In the final experiment the network learned to separate a center circle from an outer ring, reaching a test loss of 0.051 — the same geometry as an edge-ring wafer failure, where defects cluster at the wafer's rim instead of its center. Tap a building block to see what it does, and drag the slider to watch the decision boundary form.

Layers. Data flows through stacked layers; each layer re-represents the input so the next one can find structure the raw features hide — which is what lets a network draw a curved boundary around a circle.

AI Infrastructure: Training Large Language Models from Data to Deployment

A 14-slide presentation on the six-stage lifecycle that turns raw data into a deployed large language model — and what each stage consumes in data, compute, energy, and time. It compares six major models and is explicit about evidence quality: figures a lab published are marked disclosed; figures the field has had to estimate are marked estimated. That caution is earned — sources report training costs in incompatible ways, some proprietary models disclose no training compute, cost, or energy at all, and published estimates of GPT-4's training energy differ by more than 15 times. Every figure was checked against its source, with technical reports and peer-reviewed studies prioritized. The closing argument is the one my career makes natural: drawing on my wet etch process engineering at Intel, the presentation compares fab process optimization with AI training optimization — AI infrastructure is a semiconductor story, because every stage of this lifecycle runs on chips someone had to etch, inspect, and yield.

Explainable AI (XAI): Making AI Decisions Understandable

Summary. A clear, visual explainer of Explainable AI — the work of making an AI model's outputs and decisions transparent and understandable to the people who have to act on them. It matters most for large language models such as GPT, Claude, Gemini, and LLaMA when they are used in healthcare, finance, and law, where a confident answer is not enough: a clinician, analyst, or lawyer needs to know why the model said it, what it relied on, and when not to trust it.

1 · You askA question or a case is given to the model in plain language.
→
2 · The model decidesInside, the reasoning is spread across millions — even billions — of settings.
→
3 · It explainsNot just an answer, but the key factors and steps behind it, in words a person can follow.
→
4 · You can check itYou can question it, spot a weak reason, and decide whether to rely on it.

In plain language: explainable AI turns a “trust me” answer into a “here's why — check my work” answer.

Challenge Why explaining an LLM is hard

OBSTACLE 1

Opacity & complexity — the black box

There is no single rule to point to. A decision is distributed across millions or billions of parameters, so even the people who built the model cannot easily trace one answer to one clear reason.

OBSTACLE 2

Post-hoc explanations may not be faithful

An explanation written after the answer can sound convincing without reflecting the model's true internal reasoning. If people trust a tidy story that isn't accurate, the explanation itself undermines trust.

OBSTACLE 3

Data quality & bias

Models learn from their training data. If that data is incomplete, outdated, or biased, the model can repeat those patterns — and a polished explanation can hide the problem instead of revealing it.

Approach How the industry is improving transparency

Attention visualization & probing

Attention visualization shows which words or parts of the input the model paid most attention to. Probing goes a step further, testing what patterns the model has actually picked up inside — so researchers can check whether it is focusing on what should matter.

What did it focus on?Does that focus make sense?

Chain-of-thought prompting

Instead of asking only for a final answer, you ask the model to explain its reasoning step by step. That visible chain is easier for a non-technical reader to follow, question, and catch mistakes in — while remembering it still needs to be checked for faithfulness.

“Show your reasoning, step by step”

This is an active, shared effort across the field. The labs behind the models in this portfolio — OpenAI, Meta, Google, and Anthropic — are all working on ways to make their systems more interpretable, better documented, and safer to evaluate.

Trustworthy AI Validation & performance metrics

An explanation is only useful if the model underneath it is reliable. Validation is the checking process: testing the model on cases it hasn't seen, comparing its answers with known-correct outcomes, and re-testing after changes — so performance is evidence, not a claim.

Accuracy & reliabilityHow often is it right, and does it stay right across different kinds of questions and cases?
Faithfulness of the explanationDoes the explanation match what actually drove the answer, or is it just a plausible story?
ConsistencyDoes it give compatible answers and reasons when the same question is asked in slightly different ways?
Fairness & bias checksDoes performance hold up across different groups of people, or does it drop — or change tone — for some?
RobustnessDoes it cope with incomplete, messy, or unusual inputs, or does it fail confidently?
Human reviewDo domain experts — clinicians, analysts, lawyers — judge the answers and explanations as sound and usable?

Reflection What I learned / why it matters

A good explanation has to do three jobs at once: be understandable to a non-expert, be faithful to what the model actually did, and be testable. I learned that transparency is not a cosmetic add-on — in healthcare, finance, and law it is the difference between a tool a professional can defend using and one they should not rely on. It connects directly to the rest of my work: just as yield integration asks which process step caused this defect? instead of accepting the final wafer result, explainable AI asks what drove this answer? instead of accepting the final output.
