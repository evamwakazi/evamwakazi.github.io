Pre-Trained Model Decision Matrix

Seven widely used models across language, vision, and tabular data, compared on the three things that actually decide a deployment: how big the model is, how accurate it is, and how fast it answers — plus how well anyone can explain what it did.

Eva Mwakazi, Ph.D. · Semiconductor Process Engineer M.S. AI & Data Analytics, Indiana Wesleyan University (expected Aug 2027) evamwakazi.github.io

Why model selection is a trade-off, not a leaderboard

There is no “best” pre-trained model. There is only the best fit for a task, a device, a latency budget, and an audience that has to trust the output. A model that wins a benchmark can still be the wrong choice if it is too large to run where the work happens, too slow for the person waiting on it, or too opaque for a regulated decision.

Explainability is where the trade-off becomes concrete. In general, the largest models are the least inspectable: a frontier language model’s answer cannot be traced to a single feature, while a gradient-boosted tree can report exactly which variables pushed a prediction up or down. That does not make the smaller model “better.” It means accuracy, size, speed, and explainability have to be weighed together, for each domain, before anything is deployed. This matrix is a tool for doing that weighing deliberately instead of by habit.

The rule this matrix follows

Start from the constraint, not the model. If the decision must be explained to an auditor, a clinician, or a customer, explainability is the first filter. If the model must run on a phone or a fab-floor camera, size and latency are the first filter. Only then compare accuracy among the models that survive.

Methodology

How the seven models were chosen, and where every number comes from.

Selection

Two models per domain where the contrast is instructive, plus two deliberate extras. NLP / Generative AI: BERT-base, its distilled variant DistilBERT (included to make the size–accuracy trade measurable), and a GPT-4-class model as the frontier reference point. Vision: MobileNetV3 and EfficientNet-B0. Tabular: XGBoost and LightGBM. Each is a model a practitioner is genuinely likely to shortlist today, not a historical curiosity.

Model notes

Strengths, limits, and the explainability caveat that matters for each model.

GPT-4-class LLM

NLP / GEN AI
SizeUndisclosed
AccuracyMMLU 86.4%
AccessAPI only

Strength: one model covers drafting, reasoning, code, and translation with no task-specific training. Limit: cost, latency, and data leaving your systems on every call. Explainability caveat: a fluent rationale is not a faithful one — the model can produce a convincing explanation that does not reflect how the answer was produced, so high-stakes outputs still need human verification and source checking.

BERT-base

NLP / GEN AI
Size110M params
AccuracyGLUE 79.6
SpeedFast

Strength: predictable, self-hostable, and excellent once fine-tuned on a defined task. Limit: understands text but does not generate it; each new task needs labelled data and fine-tuning. Explainability caveat: attention maps look like explanations but research (Jain & Wallace, 2019) shows they can disagree with the features that actually drove the prediction — use probing and ablation alongside them.

DistilBERT

NLP / GEN AI
Size66M params
Accuracy~97% of BERT
Speed~60% faster

Strength: the clearest worked example of the size–accuracy trade in this matrix: 40% fewer parameters and roughly 60% faster inference in exchange for a small, measured accuracy loss. Limit: inherits BERT’s task-by-task fine-tuning cost. Lesson: distillation, quantisation, and pruning should be evaluated before accepting a larger model’s footprint.

EfficientNet-B0

VISION
Size5.3M params
Accuracy77.1% top-1
FamilyB0–B7

Strength: compound scaling gives a dial, not a guess — move from B0 to B7 as the accuracy budget grows, accepting 66M parameters and far more compute at B7 for 84.3% top-1. Limit: the higher variants lose the efficiency that makes the family attractive. Explainability caveat: Grad-CAM highlights the region used; it does not establish that the region was used for the right reason.

MobileNetV3 Large

VISION
Size5.4M params
Accuracy75.2% top-1
SpeedMobile CPU

Strength: designed backwards from a latency target on phone hardware, so its speed claim is the point of the architecture, not an afterthought. Limit: gives up about two points of ImageNet accuracy to EfficientNet-B0 for that latency. Best use: anywhere the camera and the decision live on the same device.

XGBoost & LightGBM

TABULAR
SizeKB – low MB
AccuracyTop-tier tabular
SpeedVery fast

Strength: on rows-and-columns data they routinely match or beat deep learning at a fraction of the size, training cost, and energy — and every prediction can be decomposed into per-feature SHAP contributions. Difference: LightGBM’s leaf-wise growth usually trains faster on large data; XGBoost’s level-wise growth can be more conservative on small, noisy sets. Limit: neither reads raw text or images; features must be engineered first.

Analysis & recommendations by domain

NLP / Generative AI

  • Generate or reason openly? GPT-4-class — with human review, because its explanations cannot be trusted as evidence.
  • Classify at volume? DistilBERT first; step up to BERT-base only if an evaluation on your own data shows the extra accuracy is real.
  • Never pay frontier-model cost and latency for a fixed label set a 66M model handles.

Computer Vision

  • On-device or real-time? MobileNetV3 — latency was its design target.
  • Server-side, accuracy-sensitive? EfficientNet, scaled B0 upward only as far as measured gains justify.
  • For wafer and surface inspection, fine-tune either backbone on fab images; ImageNet scores rank the backbones, they do not predict defect-detection performance.

Tabular Data

  • Default: LightGBM for large or frequently retrained data, XGBoost for smaller sets — both with SHAP reporting built into the deliverable.
  • Choose these first whenever the decision must be explained feature by feature: yield prediction, risk scoring, process optimisation.
  • Reach for a neural model on tabular data only with evidence it beats the boosted-tree baseline.

The same matrix, applied to a fab

Semiconductor work contains all three domains at once. Wafer inspection is a vision problem where a MobileNet- or EfficientNet-class backbone fits the camera’s compute budget. Yield and defect prediction from process parameters is a tabular problem where LightGBM or XGBoost wins on speed and gives engineers the per-variable explanation they need to act. Summarising shift logs and maintenance notes is a language problem where a GPT-4-class model drafts and a process engineer verifies. Picking one “AI strategy” for all three would get at least two of them wrong.

Conclusion

Across seven models and three domains, the pattern is consistent: accuracy is bought with size, size is paid for in speed, energy, and opacity — and the exchange rate differs by domain. Tabular models break the pattern most favourably, delivering top-tier accuracy in kilobytes with feature-level explanations. Distilled and mobile-first models show the trade can be renegotiated with architecture. Frontier language models sit at the extreme: unmatched capability, undisclosed size, and explanations that must be verified rather than believed.

The practical implication is a selection discipline: name the binding constraint first (explainability, latency, device, or capability), filter on it, then optimise accuracy among what remains — and record the evidence label on every number used to decide. That discipline, more than any single model choice, is what makes a deployment defensible.
